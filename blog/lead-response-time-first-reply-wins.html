The First Business to Reply Usually Gets the Job

Taiye Kotiku · August 14, 2026 · 6 min read

A plumber gets a WhatsApp message at 9:40 on a Tuesday morning, while both hands are under somebody else's sink. He sees it at 1:15 in the afternoon, replies properly, apologises for the delay and offers a slot the next morning. The answer comes back in under a minute: sorry, already got someone out. Nothing about that exchange was unprofessional. He lost the job at 9:41.

This is the least dramatic way a service business loses money, and by a distance the most common. Not a bad quote, not a bad review, not a competitor with better marketing. Just a gap between when someone asked and when somebody answered.

Nobody Contacts Just One Business

When a boiler stops working or a tooth starts hurting or a booking needs moving, almost nobody researches. They open Google Maps or Instagram, find two or three businesses that look plausible, and message all of them. That is not disloyalty, it is a reasonable hedge against being ignored, and most people do it without thinking about it.

Which means an enquiry is not an application waiting patiently in a queue. It is one leg of a race the customer never mentioned they had started. The other businesses in that race are not better than you. They are just also in it, and one of them will reply first.

What Slow Means to the Person Waiting

Business owners and customers measure the same delay on completely different scales. Four hours, to the owner, is a normal working morning with a job in the middle of it. Four hours, to the person who sent the message, is long enough to conclude the business is closed, overwhelmed, or no longer trading.

The reason is that chat carries the expectations of personal messaging, not of business correspondence. People send WhatsApp and Instagram messages to friends and get answers in minutes. When a business takes half a day on the same app, it does not read as busy. It reads as unreliable, and unreliable is a genuinely rational thing to avoid when you are choosing who to let into your house or trust with your teeth.

Email is judged more gently, which is exactly why so many enquiries that arrive by chat get treated with email-shaped urgency and quietly die.

Where the Time Actually Goes

The delay is almost never carelessness. It is structural, and it comes from a small number of very ordinary places.

  • You were working. The people who are best at the job are, by definition, in the middle of doing it when the enquiry lands.
  • It arrived on a channel nobody watches. A website form going to an inbox that gets checked at the end of the week, an Instagram message request sitting behind a filter, a Facebook page that nobody has the password to any more.
  • It arrived outside opening hours. Evenings and weekends are when people actually have time to sort out their admin, which is precisely when most service businesses are unreachable.
  • It arrived while you were driving. Or holding a drill, or with a client in the room, or in the twenty minutes you get to eat something.

None of these are fixed by trying harder or caring more. They are fixed by something other than a person being the thing that responds first.

What the fast version looks like

Someone messages a dental practice on Instagram at 21:40 on a Saturday about a chipped tooth. Within a minute they have a reply that acknowledges the specific problem, asks whether there is pain right now and whether they have been to the practice before, and offers the first two open slots on Monday. They take the earlier one. On Monday morning the practice does not open an unread message. It opens a booked appointment with the background already gathered.

A Realistic Target, Not a Fantasy One

The goal is not that a human being answers every message in thirty seconds forever. That is not achievable and pretending otherwise is how businesses end up buying something that fails in week three.

The achievable version separates two things that usually get treated as one: the speed of the first contact, and the speed of the resolution. Only the first one has to be immediate. A customer will happily wait until Monday for a definite answer, as long as they knew on Saturday night that somebody had them.

What sits behind that is unglamorous: every channel funnelled into one place, an automated first response that asks the same qualifying questions you would ask, a booking link or calendar slot offered while the person is still holding their phone, and a clean handover to a human the moment the conversation needs one.

Common Questions

How fast is fast enough?

Fast enough that the customer has not already started a conversation somewhere else, which in practice means a minute or two rather than a few hours. The first reply does not have to answer everything, it has to arrive before attention moves on.

Does an instant reply look automated?

It looks automated when it reads like a template that ignores what was asked. Speed is not the tell, generic content is. A reply that lands in twenty seconds and engages with the actual question reads as an organised business.

What if I cannot answer the real question straight away?

Most enquiries do not need the full answer immediately. They need proof that a real business is on the other end, and the obvious qualifying questions asked so nothing is wasted later. Acknowledge, qualify, then hand over to a human once the conversation is worth their time.

Does this apply to phone calls too?

Yes, and usually more sharply. A message left unanswered still sits in a thread the customer can return to. A call that rings out leaves nothing behind at all.
